Glamour Gram · XII

The Way Out Looks Wrong

Some problems are deceptive: every step toward the answer leads into a trap. Einstein, Maxwell and Dijkstra played their way out. Evolution does it with no mind at all.

They played

Combinatory play

In 1945 the mathematician Jacques Hadamard asked scientists how their thinking felt from the inside. Einstein wrote back that his ideas came first as images, and as something like feelings in the muscles, and only later as words. Then he named the engine.

“Combinatory play seems to be the essential feature in productive thought.”Albert Einstein, letter to Jacques Hadamard, 1945

Ninety years earlier, James Clerk Maxwell, aged 23, was studying colour vision with a spinning top. He laid discs of vermilion, emerald green and ultramarine paper over one another, spun them until they blurred, and slid the sectors until the blur matched a ring of some other colour. The same three could be balanced against every colour he tried, and the matches made by different people, in his words, “agree in a remarkable manner” (Maxwell 1855). His toy was showing that the eye reports every colour with just three kinds of sensation, as Thomas Young had proposed half a century before.

The outer ring is red, green and blue, with black filling the rest. Spun fast, the eye averages its sectors into one colour; the screen shows the average it would make (the Talbot–Plateau law). These are your screen's own three primaries, not Maxwell's papers, so the numbers differ from his, but the surprise is the same one. Red and green light make yellow, though red and green paint make mud. Three numbers match every centre here because your retina reports three numbers.

A fable · I

Gilt and Wren were apprentices in a tower of books. Gilt copied the catalogue, a page an hour. Wren spun the inkwell lids on the table to watch their labels blur. By evening Gilt had eight pages. Wren had found that three lids, red, green and blue, could be spun into the colour of every book on the shelf.

Twenty minutes, no pencil

Dijkstra's café

In 1956 Edsger Dijkstra needed a problem to show off a new computer, the ARMAC, to an audience who weren't computer people: the shortest road from Rotterdam to Groningen. He solved it sitting on a café terrace in Amsterdam with his fiancée, without pencil and paper. “It was a twenty-minute invention,” he said in 2001. He published it in 1959 (Dijkstra 1959; Frana 2001). Variations on it now route maps, networks, and the monsters chasing you in games.

The whole idea fits in one breath: always settle the nearest place you haven't settled yet. The cave below is grown from a recipe on RogueBasin, the roguelike makers' wiki. Scatter rock at random, then let each square turn to rock if most of its neighbours are rock, and repeat a few times. Tap the cave to stand somewhere else.

Each open square costs one step, and moss costs three. With moss slow, the frontier bends around it, which is what makes this Dijkstra's algorithm rather than a plain flood. Settled squares are coloured by their distance, from teal when near to gold when far. The rule that grows the cave: a square becomes rock if 5 or more of its 8 neighbours are rock, and stays rock if 4 or more are (RogueBasin). Small pockets cut off from the rest are filled in. Games usually run A*, which is Dijkstra's algorithm plus a guess at the remaining distance (Hart, Nilsson & Raphael 1968).

A fable · II

The tower's map was lost. Gilt took the first stair he saw and kept climbing. Wren tied a thread to the door and walked out one room at a time, always to the nearest room she hadn't yet reached, and chalked its distance on the wall. By supper she knew the shortest way to every room in the tower. Gilt knew one stair very well.

The deceptive maze

The way out looks wrong

In 2008 Joel Lehman and Kenneth Stanley put evolving robots in a maze whose exit lay just beyond a wall. Robots bred for getting closer to the exit crowded into the pocket beneath it, the nearest place they could reach. Robots bred only for ending up somewhere new, with no interest in the exit at all, found it in significantly fewer tries (Lehman & Stanley 2011).

To reach this exit you have to move away from it first, and every step of the way round looks worse than the pocket. That is what makes a problem deceptive. Race the two kinds of evolution below. Both breed from the same kind of robot; only what they reward differs.

Both families start from random robots.

A toy of the experiment, not the experiment. Each robot is a point that follows 18 headings, and each generation keeps the best quarter and mutates them. Gold scores a robot by how near it ends to the exit. Teal scores it by how far it ends from the 15 nearest places any robot has ended, including a remembered sample of earlier ones. Lehman and Stanley evolved neural networks steering simulated robots with range-finders, in mazes of their own. The deceptive shape is the same idea. “Run 20 races” plays twenty fresh races, up to 300 generations each, and reports what actually happened.

Novelty has a trap of its own. An agent rewarded for surprise can get stuck staring at a television that shows random static, because noise is always new (Burda et al. 2018). The kinder kind of curiosity rewards what you are learning, not what surprises you, and so it loses interest in static, where there is nothing to learn (Oudeyer, Kaplan & Hafner 2007). Babies seem to work this way already. At seven and eight months they look away from sequences that are too predictable, and from those that are too random (Kidd, Piantadosi & Aslin 2012).

A fable · III

Near the top, a window showed the summit, close enough to touch. Gilt pressed his nose to the glass and stayed there, nearer the summit than anyone, for a year. Wren wandered off down the one corridor nobody had walked, which led down, and away, and round. It came out on the summit.

Algorithms all the way down?

Evolution plays

Evolution is a search with no goal but staying around, and it out-invents engineers. In 2006 three small NASA satellites, Space Technology 5, flew with an antenna evolved by a computer: a bent wire no engineer would have drawn, whose evolved designs beat the one drawn by hand. When the mission's orbit changed, the team evolved a new antenna in a few weeks (Hornby et al. 2011). On Picbreeder, people bred pictures by picking whatever looked interesting, and found a skull and a butterfly. When the same algorithm was set the goal of evolving that skull, it couldn't (Woolley & Stanley 2011).

So is it algorithms all the way down? In 1985 David Deutsch conjectured that a single universal computer can simulate any physical system. If he is right, every process in nature is an algorithm in that sense (Deutsch 1985). It is a conjecture, not a theorem. He and Chiara Marletto have since asked a different question, which they call constructor theory: not what happens, but which transformations the laws of physics allow and which they forbid (Deutsch 2013; Marletto 2021). Page I's motto is its short form: anything not forbidden can be done, given the right knowledge. Constructor theory is a research programme, not settled physics. Sort these the way physics does.

A fable · IV

Every night the tower changed. Corridors split, and the ones nobody walked crumbled. No one designed it. Each morning the walked rooms were still there, with some new ones beside them. After a thousand years the tower held rooms no architect could have drawn, and every one of them had been walked.

The turn

Stepping stones don't look like the goal

Stanley and Lehman turned the maze into a book, Why Greatness Cannot Be Planned (2015). Vacuum tubes were made for radios. ENIAC, the first general-purpose electronic computer, ran on nearly 18,000 of them. Their point is that someone aiming at computers would never have thought to work on radio valves. The stepping stone looked nothing like the place it led.

Play is how a mind takes steps that don't pay off yet. It needs a place where looking wrong for a while is safe. Johan Huizinga called the space set apart for play a magic circle (1938), and young rats stop play-fighting when a collar a cat has worn is nearby (Siviy et al. 2010). The paediatrician and psychoanalyst Donald Winnicott spent his working life watching children play, and put it plainly.

“It is in playing and only in playing that the individual child or adult is able to be creative and to use the whole personality.”D. W. Winnicott, Playing and Reality, 1971
Try this week
  • Give one stuck problem twenty minutes with no pencil and no aim but “what happens if…”. Dijkstra's twenty minutes are still the most famous.
  • Cut a paper disc, colour half of it red and half green, and spin it on a pencil stub. Compare the colour with what the two paints make mixed in a saucer.
  • Follow one interest for no reason at all, and write down where it has led you in a month.
A fable · V

Years later, Gilt asked Wren how she always found the way. “I wasn't looking for it,” she said. “I was looking for somewhere I hadn't been.”

When every step toward the answer leads into the trap, the way out looks wrong. Play is how you take it anyway.
