The Editable Body

We can already mend a single DNA letter in a baby and wake a sleeping gene in the blood. Climb from the edits we have made to the ones nothing but our own ignorance forbids.

Easiest to edit
Rung 1 · done, in a baby

One letter

In February 2025 a baby known as KJ was given a medicine built for him alone. He was born with CPS1 deficiency: a single wrong letter in one gene left his liver unable to clear ammonia, which is toxic as it builds. A team designed a base editor, a protein that flips one DNA letter to another without cutting the strand, and delivered it to his liver inside fat droplets. The therapy was designed and made within about six months of his diagnosis. Since the infusions he has tolerated more dietary protein and needed less medication (Musunuru et al. 2025). This is one infant, with short follow-up, and the first personalized gene-editing treatment of its kind.

Still in reach
Rung 2 · approved

One gene, lift the brake

Sickle-cell disease is also one letter, in the haemoglobin gene, but the first approved CRISPR cure never touches it. Casgevy, approved in the United States in December 2023, edits a different gene: a brake called BCL11A, in a patient's own blood stem cells. Lift the brake and the body switches on the fetal haemoglobin gene, the one we all run on before birth, and the red cells stop sickling. In the trial, 15 of 16 patients went at least a year without a pain crisis (FDA 2023). One patient in the wider programme later developed a blood cancer, a known risk of the procedure.

A different control layer
Rung 3 · shown, not in people

The shape layer

Climb to the shape of the body and the control changes. It is not only in the genes. It is partly held in a pattern of voltage across the cells, the same kind of electrical difference a nerve uses, but standing still and marking out the plan. Michael Levin's group briefly disrupted that pattern in cut planarian flatworms. About a quarter grew two heads, and with their DNA untouched they kept regrowing two heads when cut again months later. Inverting the voltage set them back to one head (Durant et al. 2017). In a separate study, a 24-hour dose of drugs and signals under a wearable cap started an adult frog regrowing a working leg over 18 months (Murugan et al. 2022). These are flatworms and frogs, not people.

Paint the voltage at each end, then cut the worm and let it regrow. The shape follows the pattern, and the pattern remembers.

Harder: spread too thin to switch
Rung 4 · mapped, barely editable

Ten thousand tiny dials

Some traits are not one edit, or ten. Height is smeared across the whole genome. It took a study of 5.4 million people to map 12,111 common DNA variants that, taken together, still explain under half of the variation in height, and far less outside European-ancestry groups (Yengo et al. 2022). To edit height you would not flip a switch. You would nudge ten thousand tiny dials, each worth a fraction of a millimetre, and the gains would shrink as you went, because the biggest dials are used up first and much of the variation isn't in these variants at all.

Hardest: not yet read
Rung 5 · the known unknowns

The parts we can't read

The top rung is not a trait at all. It is the gap in the manual. The simplest cell that lives and copies itself on its own, built by stripping a bacterium to the bone, runs on 473 genes. And 149 of them are essential with no known function (Hutchison et al. 2016). We keep the cell alive without knowing what keeps it alive. A separate effort, the Unknome, ranks the genes we understand least and keeps finding that many still matter for survival and fertility (Rocha et al. 2023). You cannot edit with intent what you have not yet learned to read.

The turn

The tablet, and the real limit

In the television series Westworld, a character named Maeve rewrites herself by pulling up a tablet: every part labelled, every setting a slider she can slide. That is the top of this ladder, and the climb toward it is real, not fiction. But look at what changes as you climb. Low down, the obstacle is mechanical: can we cut the letter, can we deliver it. We can. Higher up it stops being about cutting and becomes about control: is the trait in one place or ten thousand, does the shape live in the genes or in the voltage above them, and have we read the part at all.

So, to your question: if we knew everything about biology, could we do almost everything? The laws of physics permit a great deal. Page I's motto is the short form: anything not forbidden can be done, given the right knowledge. The hardest edits on this ladder are not forbidden by physics. Most are waiting on understanding. Knowing the biology is not quite the same as being able to steer it, but it is most of the distance, and we are early in the reading.

Speculation, flagged

Would full knowledge grant near-total control? Not certainly. "Knowing everything" is a limit no field has reached, and some systems may be easier to understand than to steer without harm: a brain mid-thought, an ecosystem, a body that fights the change. There is also a gap between knowing what a part does and being able to set it safely in a living person. That is a conjecture, not a finding.

Try this week
  • Look up one gene you carry and read what it is for, and whether anyone actually knows. The Unknome database ranks the least understood.
  • Read why the sickle-cell cure lifts a brake on a backup gene instead of mending the broken one. The backup is the body's own, switched off at birth.
  • Watch a time-lapse of a planarian regrowing a head, and ask where the plan for "head" is stored.

The body is already editable. The limit isn't the cell. It's how much of it we have learned to read.
