The Price of a Mind

Your body runs a mind on about 100 watts. What would a machine need? It depends on something nobody knows yet.

Instrument · bus versus mesh

Computers spend their energy moving

In 1945 John von Neumann described the design nearly every computer still uses: memory in one place, a processor in another, and a channel between them. Every calculation means fetching data across that channel and sending the result back. In 1977 John Backus named the channel the von Neumann bottleneck.

Below, both machines do the same job. A signal moves across a grid of cells. Every cell keeps updating itself from what it senses plus a fading average of itself and its four neighbours, so the signal glows and spreads. The von Neumann machine fetches each cell's values across the bus, one cell per clock tick. The mesh updates every cell at once, in place, the way tissue does.

The ring shows where the signal really is. Both pictures start out right. Watch which one keeps up, and move your pointer or finger over the grid to steer the signal.

Energy figures are Mark Horowitz's measurements for a 45 nm chip (2014), as tabulated by Song Han and colleagues (2016). Reading 32 bits from main memory costs about 640 pJ, and from a small nearby memory about 5 pJ. A 32-bit floating-point add costs 0.9 pJ and a multiply 3.7 pJ. For one full update of this grid, the von Neumann machine spends about 645 nJ moving data and needs 144 ticks. The mesh spends about 5 nJ in one tick. Both spend under 2 nJ on the maths itself. Real computers soften this with caches and many cores, but the gap is why the problem has a name.

Two ideas from the frontier

Life doesn't run software, it is the computation

PolycomputingJosh Bongard and Michael Levin argue that living matter computes many things at once in the same place, and which computation you see depends on who is observing. One tissue can be a pump, a signal, a memory and a sensor all at the same time. Bongard's lab has built materials that compute different logic functions at different vibration frequencies simultaneously.Bongard & Levin, 2023
Pancomputational enactivismMichael Timothy Bennett argues that treating a mind as software running on separate hardware, which he calls computational dualism, makes claims about intelligence depend on an arbitrary interpreter. His alternative treats the whole embodied system, interacting with its world, as the computation.Bennett, 2023–2024

In your body, nothing waits for a bus. Every synapse stores and computes in the same place. Ion channels, hormones, heartbeat, gut, immune system and the bioelectric fields between cells all update at once, at every scale, and each level both reads and rewrites the others. Your brain does it on roughly 20 watts.

Are these ideas proven? No. They are frameworks, argued seriously, that point at a real difference between how we build machines and how life works.

Instrument · the price list

What would it cost to run a mind?

To run a mind on a machine you have to choose how much detail to simulate. Abstract neural computation is cheap. Simulating every molecule is not. Choose a level of detail, a number of minds and a hardware efficiency, and see what power it takes.

–

    Levels 2 to 7 use Anders Sandberg and Nick Bostrom's estimates for emulating a human brain (2008). Level 1 is Joseph Carlsmith's central estimate (2020). Level 8 is our own rough estimate: about 7 × 10²⁷ atoms, stepped every femtosecond, with around a thousand operations per atom per step. It still leaves out quantum effects and the world the body lives in. Reversible computing could in principle dip below the Landauer limit.

    What this means for AI

    Will today's AI hit a wall?

    Large language models run on chips that are massively parallel but still shuttle billions of numbers between memory and processor for every word they write. Chip arithmetic has grown far faster than memory bandwidth, a gap called the memory wall since 1995 that has only widened (Gholami et al., 2024). Data centres used about 1.5 percent of the world's electricity in 2024, and the International Energy Agency expects that to roughly double by 2030.

    There are counter-currents. The compute needed to reach a given level of language-model performance has been halving roughly every eight months through better algorithms (Ho et al., 2024). And new chips such as IBM's NorthPole and Intel's Loihi move memory and computing together, closer to how tissue works.

    So whether today's architecture reaches a ceiling before it matches the full range of what minds do is an open question. The answer depends on the problem below.

    An open problem

    The price-of-a-mind problem

    Stated plainly

    Let g* be the coarsest level of detail at which a simulation of a person still produces a mind like theirs, and possibly their experience. Let C(g) be the operations per second needed at level g, and E the energy per operation. Then hosting N minds takes power

    P = N × C(g*) × E

    What we know: C ranges from about 10¹⁵ to 10⁴⁶ operations per second depending on g. E ranges from about 10⁻¹² joules today down to 3 × 10⁻²¹ at the Landauer limit for irreversible computing.

    What nobody knows: g*. Nor do we know whether experience comes along at any level, or whether a mind even has a level apart from its body and its world, as enactivists argue.

    Why it matters: for eight billion minds the answer lands anywhere from less power than humanity uses today to more than the output of hundreds of millions of Suns. The Sun could power all of them on today's chips only if a mind at g* needs fewer than about 5 × 10²⁸ operations per second.

    Meanwhile, every one of us runs at g*, whatever it is, on about 100 watts, assembled from food. Biology is not just efficient by our standards. It may be doing a different kind of thing.

    One more consequence, and it cuts both ways. If g* is coarse, digital minds will be cheap. Then so might digital suffering be, multiplied quietly by the billion. Knowing g* may be one of the most important things we could learn before we build.

    A mind might cost a light bulb or a galaxy. We don't yet know which.
