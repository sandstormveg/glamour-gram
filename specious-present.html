The Specious Present

Your "now" is assembled after the fact, and it is wider than an instant.

Experiment 1 · the flash-lag effect

The flash falls behind

Watch the moving dot. Every so often a bar flashes right next to it. Most people see the bar trailing behind the dot. It isn't. Turn on "Freeze the flash" to check.

David Eagleman and Terrence Sejnowski showed in 2000 that what you see at the moment of the flash depends on what the dot does in the next eighty milliseconds or so. Your brain waits, gathers evidence, then writes the past. They called it postdiction.

Experiment 2 · color phi

A change you saw before it happened

Two dots flash in turn: an orange one on the left, then a teal one on the right. At the right timing you see one dot gliding across and changing color halfway. But nothing appeared in between. To show the change midway, your brain had to know the second color before the second dot arrived.

Try 50 to 150 ms for smooth motion. Past about 300 ms the illusion breaks into two separate flashes. Effects vary from person to person and screen to screen.

Paul Kolers and Michael von Grünau reported this in 1976. The philosopher Daniel Dennett used it to argue that there is no single moment where experience "happens." The brain keeps drafts and settles on a story.

Experiments 3 and 4 · with sound

How wide is your now?

Start the beats, then slowly widen the gap. Notice the point where the beats stop feeling like a rhythm and become separate events. For most people it is around two to three seconds. The psychologist Ernst Pöppel argued that this window is the width of the present moment.

At fast speeds, identical beats start to sound grouped: tick-TOCK, tick-TOCK. Nothing in the sound changes. Your brain adds the accent, an effect Thaddeus Bolton described in 1894.

Now test what counts as "at the same time." A flash and a beep play together, offset by the amount you choose. Find where they stop feeling simultaneous.

Negative means the beep comes first. Most people accept a late beep more easily than an early one, since in nature sound always arrives after light. Your screen and speakers add delays of their own, so treat these numbers as rough.

What this means

The present is a saddle-back

"The practically cognized present is no knife-edge, but a saddle-back, with a certain breadth of its own on which we sit perched, and from which we look in two directions into time."

William James, The Principles of Psychology, 1890

The phrase "specious present" comes from E. R. Clay in 1882, and James made it famous. Since then, the experiments have filled in the details. You live roughly a tenth of a second in the past, while your brain finishes the edit. Events within a few tens of milliseconds get bound together into one moment. And moments within about three seconds get bound into one "now."

Physics, in Einstein's block universe, has no moving present at all. The flow you feel may be built here, in this window, out of waiting and editing and binding. If so, a "now" isn't something you are in. It's something you make.

You don't live in the present. You compose it.
