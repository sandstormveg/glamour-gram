Everyone Like You

No message, no second meeting, no way to check. Copies can still cooperate, because what you choose is a clue to what everyone like you chooses. Genes worked it out first.

A dilemma

A dilemma with yourself

You're alone in a room with a dollar. You can keep it, or send it to someone in another room, and money you send triples on the way. They have the same choice. You'll never meet, never talk, and never play again.

Whatever the other room does, keeping leaves you a dollar better off. That's the prisoner's dilemma, and against a stranger the textbook answer is to keep.

Now change one thing. The other room holds an exact copy of you, made a minute ago, with your memories and your habits of thought. It's weighing the same dollar with the same mind. Do you still keep it?

Each round, the other room holds a copy of you with the chance you set. A copy makes your choice. Otherwise it holds a stranger, who tosses a coin. The two lines are what each choice earns on average at every setting. Nothing passes through the wall until both have chosen.

Slide toward a stranger and watch the lines cross. Sending pays as soon as the other room holds a copy more than one time in three.

But the first argument hasn't gone away. Hold the other room's choice fixed, and keeping always earns one dollar more. The question is whether you're allowed to hold it fixed. Causal decision theory says yes: your choice can't reach through the wall, so take the extra dollar. Evidential decision theory, after Richard Jeffrey (1965), says no: your choice is evidence about the other room's, so choose the world you'd rather find yourself in. Against a stranger the two theories agree. Against a copy they part.

Douglas Hofstadter argued in 1983 that people who are equally rational, and know it, should see that they'll reason alike, and so cooperate. He called it superrationality. Then he put a one-shot dilemma, for real money, to twenty friends. Fourteen defected.

Natural selection

Evolution sides with the evidence

This sounds like a puzzle for philosophers. It's older than philosophers. A gene sits in many bodies at once, so a gene that makes a body help its relatives is helping copies of itself, in rooms it will never see.

In 1964 W. D. Hamilton worked out when such a gene spreads: when r × b > c. The help is worth b to the one who gets it and costs c to the one who gives it, and r is their relatedness. Relatedness sounds like family, but in the modern theory it's a statistic: how well the giver's genes predict the receiver's (Grafen 1985). Family is just the commonest way to make that prediction good.

Put in the dollar game's numbers, a cost of 1 and a gift of 3, and Hamilton's rule says r > 1/3. That's exactly where the lines crossed in the other room. In 1994 the philosopher Brian Skyrms showed why it's no coincidence. When like tends to meet like, natural selection scores each strategy by what it tends to meet. That's Jeffrey's evidential rule, not the dominance argument. Selection doesn't take sides in the philosophy. It counts who's left.

A toy model. Each generation, every cell meets a partner: one of its own kind with chance r, otherwise anyone. Senders pay 1 to give their partner the gift. Then cells reproduce in proportion to what they have, and offspring settle near their parents. Gold cells send, blue cells keep. Now and then a cell is born the other kind. Close to the threshold, selection is weak and luck can carry either side for a while.

Your body is the extreme case. Its roughly 30 trillion cells all grew from one fertilized egg, so they're as close to copies as cells get (Sender, Fuchs & Milo 2016). Nearly all of them give up passing on their genes and work for the few cells that will. Biologists think starting every life from a single cell is part of what keeps the cells alike enough to cooperate (Grosberg & Strathmann 2007). A cancer is a lineage of cells that has started reproducing for itself (Aktipis et al. 2015).

Some genes skip the family and check for copies directly. Hamilton imagined a gene that gives its carriers a visible tag and makes them help others with the tag. Richard Dawkins called it a green beard (1976). Green beards turned out to be real. In the social amoeba Dictyostelium, cells carrying the gene csA stick to one another as they gather into a fruiting body, and in mixed groups on soil they made 82% of the spores (Queller et al. 2003). Yeast cells with FLO1 clump into flocs that shield the cells inside from alcohol and poisons, and they stick mainly to each other (Smukalla et al. 2008).

A green beard checks the tag, not the behaviour, so a cheat can grow the beard without the kindness. Programmers have met the same weakness. When programs can read each other's code, "cooperate with exact copies of me" works, but one changed character breaks it (Tennenholtz 2004). A sturdier program cooperates with anything it can prove will cooperate back, and a theorem of logic, Löb's, lets two such programs find each other (Bárász et al. 2014).

A predictor

Two boxes and a prediction

In 1960 William Newcomb, a physicist at the Lawrence Livermore laboratory, made up a puzzle while thinking about the prisoner's dilemma. The philosopher Robert Nozick published it in 1969. Online, the predictor in it is often called Omega.

A being that predicts people's choices very well sets two boxes in front of you. Box A is glass and holds $1,000. Box B is closed. Yesterday the predictor put $1,000,000 in B if it predicted you'd take only B, and nothing if it predicted you'd take both. You can take B alone, or both. Whatever is in B is already there.

Both, on average

The boxes were filled yesterday. Choose.

The predictor reads your habits, not your click: with the accuracy you set, it foresaw whatever you're about to choose. Whatever is in B, taking both gets you $1,000 more. Taking only B pays more on average whenever the predictor beats a coin toss by a twentieth of a percentage point.

In 1979 David Lewis showed that the twin's dilemma is this puzzle in disguise. Your copy is a predictor of you, with its own dollar at stake. Lewis himself took both boxes.

People still split. When Martin Gardner put the puzzle to the readers of Scientific American in 1973, their letters favoured one box by about five to two. Newcomb was a one-boxer. In a Guardian poll in 2016, 53.5% of readers took one box. In the 2020 PhilPapers survey of philosophers, 31% took one box and 39% took two, and among decision theorists two boxes won by 73% to 21%. Two-boxers agree that one-boxers walk away richer. Lewis named a paper after the one-boxers' taunt, "Why ain'cha rich?" (1981). His answer was that the predictor reserved its riches for the irrational.

The predictor is other people

You don't need a godlike predictor to be in Newcomb's problem. You only need people who can read you a little. After half an hour of conversation, strangers predicted each other's choices in a one-shot dilemma better than chance (Frank, Gilovich & Regan 1993). The effect is real but small, and small talk alone isn't enough (Manson, Gervais & Kline 2013; Sparks, Burleigh & Barclay 2016). The philosopher David Gauthier called people like this translucent: neither transparent nor opaque (1986).

Derek Parfit made the stakes plain (1984). You're stranded in the desert at night. A driver will take you to town if they believe you'll pay them when you get there, and you're a poor liar. Once in town, paying is a pure loss. If you're the kind of person who'd reason that way, it shows on your face, and you stay in the desert.

The economist Robert Frank argued that this is what some emotions are for (1988). Guilt, gratitude and indignation push us to do what doesn't pay in the moment. Because other people can partly see them, they make us worth trusting, and that pays over a life. Robert Trivers went a step further. If others read your motives, it can pay to hide your motives even from yourself, so they don't leak (1976; von Hippel & Trivers 2011). Kevin Simler and Robin Hanson's book on the hidden motives in everyday life is called The Elephant in the Brain (2018).

Put together, these ideas suggest that conscience is how evolution takes one box. That's an interpretation, and a contested one, not a finding.

A predictor in the open

A forecast you can hear

Newcomb's predictor keeps its prediction in a closed box. Most real predictions are announced: polls, forecasts, credit scores, the next video your phone lines up. The people they predict can hear them.

Karl Popper called a prediction's influence on what it predicts the Oedipus effect, after the oracle whose prophecy set Oedipus on the road to fulfilling it (1957). Robert Merton told of a sound bank ruined by a rumour that it was failing, as everyone came for their money at once (1948). Oskar Morgenstern's example was a chase (1928). Sherlock Holmes flees Professor Moriarty by train, from London toward Dover. If Moriarty will follow him to Dover, Holmes should get off early, at Canterbury. But Moriarty can foresee that, and Holmes can foresee Moriarty foreseeing it, and so on forever.

Can an announced prediction ever be right? In 1954 Emile Grunberg and Franco Modigliani, and separately Herbert Simon, proved that it often can. If people's reactions change smoothly with what they hear, then some forecast, once announced, comes true. It's a fixed point, the same mathematics as the theorem that when you stir a cup of coffee, some point in it ends up where it started (Brouwer 1911).

A forecaster announces the chance that someone will go for a run tomorrow, and they hear it. The curve is the real chance they go, for each forecast they might hear. Each announcement uses what happened last time as the next forecast, like a model retrained on its own effects. Drag the points to reshape the person. Tap the plot to choose the first forecast.

Try to drag the curve so it never meets the diagonal. You can't, unless the curve jumps. The jump is the contrarian: someone who does the opposite of whatever they're told, the moment they hear it. For them no announced forecast can come true. Alan Turing used the same trick in 1936 to prove that no program can tell, for every program, whether it will ever stop. Hand a program the prediction about itself, and have it do the opposite.

Now pick the contrarian with a coin. If they toss a coin when the forecast is exactly 50%, then "50%" comes true. Randomness is the way out on both sides. John von Neumann proved in 1928 that in a game of pure conflict, a player who randomizes in the right proportions can't be outguessed. In 1944 he and Morgenstern worked out Holmes's best plan: get off at Canterbury with probability 60%. Even so, they noted, Holmes is "as good as 48% dead" as his train pulls out of Victoria. Forecasters learn the same lesson. A forecaster who follows a predictable rule can't stay calibrated, right 70% of the times it says 70%, against every possible run of events, because the events could be chosen to defy it (Oakes 1985). One who adds a little randomness can (Foster & Vohra 1998).

Animals found the coin first. A hunting bat is a predictor its prey can hear. In the 1960s Kenneth Roeder showed that many moths have ears with only two sensory cells. When a bat is far off, the moth turns and flies away. When the bat is close and loud, the moth's flight breaks into loops, spirals and dives. Biologists call it protean behaviour, after the shape-shifting sea god (Humphries & Driver 1970).

Machine learning has met all this again as performative prediction. A model's forecasts change the behaviour it was trained on, so retraining chases its own effects, and whether it settles depends on how hard people react (Perdomo et al. 2020). Pick "Pushes back hard": a forecast that would come true is sitting right there, and retraining never reaches it.

The predictors have a limit too. In 2008 David Wolpert proved, with the same diagonal trick, that no device inside a universe can be guaranteed to predict everything in it, whatever the laws of physics. The prediction is part of what it predicts.

So predicting someone who can see the prediction isn't impossible in general. It's impossible when they're set on proving it wrong and never hesitate. If their reaction changes smoothly, or they'll toss a coin at the tipping point, some forecast comes true. It may be hard to reach, and there may be several, including some that nobody wanted.

Physics

Nature sets a ceiling

Everything so far runs on correlation: two choices that match more often than chance, with no message between them. How strong can such a correlation be? Physics has measured the answer, with a game named CHSH after the four physicists whose 1969 inequality it's built on: John Clauser, Michael Horne, Abner Shimony and Richard Holt.

Two players are taken far apart. Each is asked a random question, 0 or 1, and must answer 0 or 1 at once, before any signal could pass between them. They win if their answers match, except when both were asked 1. Then they win if their answers differ. They can agree on any plan they like beforehand.

With a plan, each player answers from it. With photons, each player measures one photon of an entangled pair through a polarizer, turned by their question: Alice to 0° or 45°, Bob to plus or minus his tilt. This simulation cheats: to get the odds right, its code looks at both settings at once. Real photons can't, and they still win.

No plan wins more than three rounds in four. Follow the rules around a loop. Alice's answer to 0 must match Bob's answer to 0, which must match Alice's answer to 1, which must differ from Bob's answer to 1, which must match Alice's answer to 0. So Alice's answer to 0 would have to differ from itself, and at least one rule always breaks. Choosing a plan at random doesn't help, because an average of plans can't beat the best of them.

Entangled photons do better. Set Bob's tilt to 22.5° and they win about 85.4% of rounds, the most quantum mechanics allows (Tsirelson 1980). This isn't only theory. In 2015, teams in Delft, Vienna and Boulder ran it with the two main loopholes closed at once. On 30 November 2016, about 100,000 people played a video game to supply the random questions for 13 experiments on five continents (the BIG Bell Test, 2018). The 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Alain Aspect, John Clauser and Anton Zeilinger for this line of work.

The photons still can't carry a message. Each player's answers, on their own, look like coin tosses, and the pattern only shows when the two lists are laid side by side. Here is the strange part. Winning every round wouldn't carry a message either. Sandu Popescu and Daniel Rohrlich described such a perfect partnership in 1994, and nothing in relativity forbids it. Nature stops at 85.4%. Quantum mechanics sets that limit, but nobody knows a deeper reason for it. One proposal, information causality, says that any stronger correlation would let a message of n bits reveal more than n bits about the sender's data (Pawłowski et al. 2009).

The cooperation on the rest of this page runs on the ordinary kind of correlation, a shared plan: genes, upbringing, habits of mind. In this game a shared plan tops out at 75%, because the two questions are drawn separately. In the twin's dilemma both players face the same question, so a shared plan can match every time. A copy of you needs no special physics. Only a shared past.

Speculation, flagged

Everyone, everywhere

Now take the twin's dilemma out into cosmology.

If space is infinite and filled with matter roughly as it is here, then by plain probability every possible arrangement of particles turns up somewhere, again and again. Max Tegmark estimated that your nearest exact copy would be about 101028 metres away (2003). Whether space is infinite, nobody knows. Measurements say it's very nearly flat, which fits an infinite universe without proving one.

Far more common would be minds that are merely similar, shaped by the same physics and the same pressures of natural selection. Evolution repeats itself: eyes evolved separately at least forty times (Salvini-Plawen & Mayr 1977). If minds out there decide anything like the way you do, your choices are weak evidence about theirs.

In 2017 Caspar Oesterheld followed this to an odd conclusion, now called evidential cooperation in large worlds. If you give weight to the values of minds elsewhere, that's evidence that minds like you, elsewhere, give weight to yours. So everyone who reasons this way should work toward one compromise among all their values. Here at home, he argues, that favours pluralism and ordinary cooperation. Deals like this, struck with no contact at all, are sometimes called acausal trade.

How much weight it bears
  • It needs a very large universe. That's possible, not established.
  • It needs the evidential way of choosing, which most decision theorists reject.
  • It needs real correlation with minds we know almost nothing about, and some idea of what they value.
  • Its practical advice, to cooperate and keep many values in view, stands on ordinary grounds anyway. That makes it cheap to follow. It doesn't make it more likely to be true.

It sounds like Immanuel Kant: act only on a rule you could will everyone to follow (1785). But nothing here is commanded. It's arithmetic about correlated choices, and whether the arithmetic reaches across the cosmos depends on facts about physics and minds that nobody has.

The turn

The nearest copy

Your nearest copy isn't 101028 metres away. It wakes up in your bed tomorrow.

The psychiatrist George Ainslie argues that willpower is the twin's dilemma played out across time (2001). Your successive selves are separate players, each tempted in its own moment. What binds them is that each choice is evidence about the next. If you break a resolution tonight, that's a sign you'll break it every night, so tonight's choice becomes a vote on all of them. A resolution has force because a lapse is bad news about you.

Evidence can be faked, and the faker can be you. In 1984 George Quattrone and Amos Tversky had students hold an arm in cold water, then told them that people with healthy hearts tolerate cold better after exercise, or worse. On the second try, most shifted their tolerance in whichever direction meant a healthy heart. Their endurance couldn't change their hearts. David Lewis's name for this mistake is managing the news (1981).

So there's a line to draw. Choosing as if for everyone like you is sound when your choice really is being worked out in those other places: in a copy, in a predictor that read you, in tomorrow's you. It's self-deception when the other place doesn't run on your choices at all, like a heart. Functional decision theory tries to draw that line (Yudkowsky & Soares 2017). Whether it draws it in the right place is still argued.

"To believe your own thought, to believe that what is true for you in your private heart is true for all men,—that is genius."Ralph Waldo Emerson, "Self-Reliance" (1841)

Emerson meant it about genius. It's also a claim about correlation. You're never deciding only for yourself. You decide for tomorrow's you, for everyone who reasons the way you do, for everyone who can read you a little, and, if the universe is large enough, for copies you'll never meet. The twin's dilemma isn't a puzzle about strangers. It's the shape of every choice made by something that has copies.

Try this week
  • Next time you're about to break a resolution, ask what this choice predicts about the next hundred.
  • Play the dollar game with a friend. Each of you writes Send or Keep in secret, then reveal. Talk for ten minutes, and play again.
  • Notice one thing you do mainly to prove to yourself what kind of person you are. Is it a policy, or are you managing the news?
  • On a summer night, watch the moths around a light where bats are hunting. A moth that suddenly drops or spirals may just have heard one close by.

You never choose alone. Choose the way you'd want everyone like you to choose.
