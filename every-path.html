Every Path at Once

Light, a corgi and a column of ants all find the fastest way across a border. None of them can see it. Here is how they find it anyway.

A race

The shortest way is not the fastest

You're a lifeguard. Someone is in trouble, out in the water and further down the beach. You run much faster than you swim. Which way do you go?

Straight at them is the shortest path, but it spends too long in the water. Running to the point nearest them and then swimming straight out spends too long on the sand. The fastest way is in between. It bends at the waterline.

Drag the point where you reach the water, or use the slider. The curve under the beach is the total time for every crossing point, so the best crossing sits right above its lowest point. Angles a and b are measured from the line straight out to sea.

Find the bottom of the curve and compare the two numbers. At the fastest crossing, the sines of the two angles are in the same ratio as the two speeds. That's the law of refraction. Ibn Sahl wrote it down in Baghdad in 984, in a book on burning mirrors and lenses. Thomas Harriot found it again in 1602 and Willebrord Snell in 1621, and neither published. René Descartes did, in 1637. In English it's called Snell's law.

Now switch the crossing to light. Light is about a quarter slower in water than in air, and it bends by exactly this rule. That's why a straw in a glass looks broken at the surface.

An objection

How does light know?

In 1662 Pierre de Fermat showed that the law of refraction follows from one idea: light takes the path of least time. It only works if light is slower in water than in air, which nobody could check. Léon Foucault finally measured it in 1850.

The idea was beautiful, and it was a scandal. Claude Clerselier, defending Descartes, wrote back that "nature takes the quickest way" is a moral principle, not a physical one. It gives light a goal. How could a ray leaving a candle know where the water is, and how much time a detour would save?

Fermat gave ground. He offered to leave the physics to others, so long as he could keep his geometry.

The idea spread anyway. In 1744 Pierre-Louis Maupertuis proposed that nature always spends as little as it can of a quantity he called action. Leonhard Euler, Joseph-Louis Lagrange and William Rowan Hamilton turned that into one of the most powerful tools in physics. A thrown ball, a planet and a pendulum each follow the path that makes their action stationary. But for nearly three centuries nobody could say how a ball, or a ray of light, finds that path. The answer, when it came, was stranger than purpose.

The answer

Light takes every path

In 1948 Richard Feynman found a way to do quantum mechanics that never chooses a path at all. Light, or an electron, goes every possible way, and each way carries a little arrow, like the hand of a stopwatch that turns as the light travels. Add up the arrows for all the paths, head to tail. The square of the total arrow's length is how much light arrives.

Here is the trick. Far from the fastest path, a small change of route changes the travel time a lot, so neighbouring paths have arrows pointing every which way, and they cancel. Near the fastest path the time hardly changes, so neighbouring arrows agree and add up. Light only seems to choose the quickest way, because everywhere else the paths cancel each other out.

A lamp, an eye, a screen between them, and a mirror cut into 161 strips. Each strip is one path, with its own arrow. The chain adds the arrows head to tail, and the gold arrow is the total. Bright strips help, dark strips cancel, and the curve under the mirror is each path's travel time. Drag along the mirror to scrape strips away or put them back. The light's waves are drawn thousands of times too long, so the arrows can be seen.

Cover the middle and the eye goes nearly dark, though most of the mirror is still there. The ends cancel themselves. Now make a grating: keep only the left end, and scrape away every strip whose arrow points against the rest. Light reaches the eye from a part of the mirror where it "shouldn't" reflect at all. It only works for one colour. Slide the colour and it fails, which is why the back of a CD throws rainbows.

Then keep only the helpers, all along the mirror. The eye gets several times more light than the whole mirror gave it. Removing mirror made it brighter. That's a zone plate, and the same trick is used to focus X-rays, which ordinary lenses can hardly bend.

Not least, but steady

Strictly, light doesn't take the quickest path. It takes a path where a small change of route makes no difference to the time, to first order. Usually that's the quickest. Set the mirror to Bowl and the light that survives takes the slowest of its paths. Set it to Ellipse and every path takes the same time, so every arrow agrees and the mirror focuses.

What matters is not the minimum but the flatness. A path survives when its neighbours agree with it.

The same holds for everything, not only light. A thrown ball also, in this sense, takes every path, but its arrows spin so fast that only the paths right beside one path survive. That's why big things seem to follow a single route, and why Maupertuis's principle works. Clerselier was right that nature has no purpose. Fermat was right about the path.

The length of the total arrow is the same quantity that measured sync among the fireflies in The Duet: how well a crowd of little arrows agree.

Trying with feet

Ants that refract

In 2013 Jan Oettler and colleagues gave colonies of the little fire ant, Wasmannia auropunctata, a problem with a border in it. The nest sat on one surface and the food on another. On a smooth plastic sheet the ants walked at about 4.9 millimetres a second. On rough felt they managed 1.7. Over a few days the trails settled, and they didn't run straight. They bent at the border toward the angle Fermat's principle predicts for light. In 15 of 18 trials the trail was closer to Fermat's path than to the straight line.

No ant sees the whole route. The authors point to the mechanism behind the classic shortcut experiments with Argentine ants. Ants lay scent, scent fades, and quicker variants of a trail get reinforced more. The colony tries many paths with its feet and keeps the quick ones.

Trying in the head

A corgi named Elvis

In 2003 the mathematician Tim Pennings published the fetch records of his Welsh corgi, Elvis. On the shore of Lake Michigan, Pennings threw a tennis ball into the water. Elvis sprinted along the beach, plunged in at some point, and swam the rest. Pennings had timed him at 6.40 metres a second on sand and 0.91 in water. On each throw he chased Elvis with a screwdriver, pushed it into the sand where the dog went in, and measured with a tape. After three hours they had 35 throws. He left out the couple of times Elvis was too excited and swam the whole way.

The calculus says the best place to go in depends only on how far out the ball is: start swimming when the distance left along the shore is 0.144 times the ball's distance from shore. Most of Elvis's 35 choices sit close to that line. Pennings admitted the dog doesn't know calculus. He has, Pennings wrote, "trouble differentiating even simple polynomials."

C is the point on the shore nearest the ball. A gold ghost runs the fastest route beside your dog. Everything runs at three times Elvis's speed.

How does Elvis do it? In 2006 the psychologists Pierre Perruchet and Jorge Gallego, testing a Labrador named Salsa, proposed a simpler rule: at every moment, do whatever closes the gap to the ball fastest. Keep running while running closes it faster, and plunge in once swimming would. That rule needs no map and no foresight, and on a beach it lands on exactly the same point as the calculus. Press "Let the dog lean" and see.

Pennings answered with a new experiment. He stood in the water with Elvis beside him and threw a stick along the shore. Elvis swam in to the beach, ran along it, and dove back in. Swimming toward the shore doesn't close the gap to the stick at all, so a moment-to-moment rule can't do that. It looks as if Elvis chose the whole route before he set off. With Roland Minton, Pennings later worked out exactly when that detour is the fastest way (2007).

So the question is open. If Elvis plans, he's doing what light does, with far fewer paths: trying routes in his head and keeping the quick one. That's an interpretation, not a finding.

The turn

There is a right way

Here are three ways to find the quickest path without seeing it. Light tries every path at once, and the ones that disagree cancel. Ants try many paths with their feet, and fading scent keeps the quick ones. A dog may try a few in its head before it moves.

The last way is yours too. Karl Popper's case for imagination was that it lets our hypotheses die in our stead. Every route you walk through in your head and drop is one you didn't have to walk with your feet.

"There is a right way; but we are very liable from heedlessness and stupidity to take the wrong one."Henry David Thoreau, "Walking" (1862)

Light adds one more lesson, about which paths last. Not the perfect ones: the ones where a small wobble costs almost nothing. That part is a metaphor, not physics. You aren't a photon. But it makes a good test for a plan. If one small slip would ruin it, look for a flatter route.

Try this week
  • Stand a straw in a glass of water and look from the side. It breaks at the surface, because the light from below took the quickest way to your eye.
  • Tilt a CD or DVD under a lamp. Its tracks are a grating that sends each colour its own way.
  • Throw a ball for a dog on a beach, and watch where it goes in.
  • Find a worn path cutting across the corner of a lawn. People's feet did what the ants do.

Nobody sees the whole path. Try many, lightly, and keep the ones that forgive a wobble.
