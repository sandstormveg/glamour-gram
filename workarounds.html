Nature's Workarounds

Evolution has already invented things we're still dreaming of. Some are hiding in a drop of seawater.

Discovery · 2024

A bacterium that became an organ

Every protein in your body needs nitrogen. The air is 78 percent nitrogen, yet almost no living thing can use it directly. Only some bacteria and archaea can break the triple bond of N₂ and "fix" it into ammonia. Plants and animals have always depended on them.

In 2024, Tyler Coale, Jonathan Zehr and colleagues showed that a tiny ocean alga, Braarudosphaera bigelowii, has crossed that line. Long ago it swallowed a nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterium called UCYN-A. Over time the guest became part of the host. It now imports proteins made by the host's genes and divides in step with the host cell. That makes it an organelle: the nitroplast, the first known nitrogen-fixing organelle.

The host supplies carbon from photosynthesis and proteins coded in its own nucleus. The nitroplast returns usable nitrogen.

A swallowed bacterium has turned into an organelle only a handful of times in four billion years:

Why it matters: farming now leans on the Haber–Bosch process, which makes ammonia fertiliser at high heat and pressure and uses around 1–2 percent of the world's energy. Roughly half of humanity eats food grown with it. Crops with their own nitroplasts are a long way off, but nature has now shown it can be done.

Instrument · a brainless solver

One cell, millions of nuclei, no brain

The slime mould Physarum polycephalum is a single cell. Its nuclei divide without the cell dividing, so one yellow cell can hold millions of nuclei and spread wider than a dinner plate. It has no neurons, yet:

It solves mazesPlaced in a maze with food at both exits, it withdraws from dead ends and keeps the shortest path.Nakagaki, Yamada & Tóth, 2000
It designs railwaysWith oat flakes placed like the cities around Tokyo, it grew a network about as efficient and resilient as the real rail system.Tero et al., 2010
It learns and remembersIt learns to cross bitter but harmless barriers, and stores where food was in the thickness of its tubes.Boisseau et al., 2016 · Kramar & Alim, 2021
Click or tap to drop an oat flake.

A simple model of Physarum after Jeff Jones (2010): thousands of particles each follow the chemical trail ahead of them and leave a little more. Each also streams toward one oat flake and then another, the way cytoplasm shuttles through the real cell. Tubes emerge from these local rules alone.

Every hallmark has its own language

The twelve hallmarks of aging

In 2013 Carlos López-Otín and colleagues listed nine processes that drive aging. In 2023 they extended the list to twelve. Each is its own problem with its own vocabulary, and for each one, some creature somewhere has found a partial answer. Choose one.

Instrument · why aging was never fixed

Selection goes quiet with age

Evolution never "decided" that we should age. It simply can't see old age very well. In the wild, most animals die of predators, accidents or disease long before they grow old, so genes that act late in life are barely tested. Raise the danger and watch selection's attention fade sooner.

The prediction holds up in the wild. On Sapelo Island, off Georgia, opossums have lived for thousands of years with few predators. Steven Austad found they age more slowly than mainland opossums (1993).

Nothing in physics requires aging. Hydra and some turtles show almost none. Aging may be less a law than a gap that evolution never had a reason to close. That makes it a problem knowledge can work on.

Nature wrote the workarounds in pieces. We can learn to read them together.
