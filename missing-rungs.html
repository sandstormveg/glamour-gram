The Missing Rungs

Superintelligence, superhappiness, superhealth. The ladder from human to posthuman, zoomed in rung by rung: what's built, what's drawn but not yet built, and what nobody knows how to make.

Three supers

Three ways to be more

In 1957 the biologist Julian Huxley needed a word for an old hope, that people might use science to improve not only their circumstances but themselves.

"The human species can, if it wishes, transcend itself … We need a name for this new belief. Perhaps transhumanism will serve: man remaining man, but transcending himself, by realizing new possibilities of and for his human nature."Julian Huxley, "Transhumanism," New Bottles for New Wine (1957)

The philosopher Nick Bostrom gave the far end a working definition (2008). A posthuman is a being with at least one general capacity that greatly exceeds the most any human could reach without new technology. He named three such capacities: healthspan, cognition and emotion. The philosopher David Pearce calls the same three the three supers: superlongevity, superintelligence and superhappiness (The Hedonistic Imperative, 1995). The futurist FM-2030 used "transhuman" for the stretch in between: a transitional human, on the way (1989).

This page treats the three supers as engineering problems. Each gets a ladder, from today at the bottom to posthuman at the top, and every rung, and every step inside a rung, is sorted into one of four kinds.

BuiltbuiltIn use, in people.
ShownshownIt works in animals, in a lab, or in a handful of people.
DrawndrawnWorked out on paper, or shown possible in principle. Nobody has built it.
MissingmissingNobody knows how yet.

Where a rung sits is a judgement from the published evidence as of October 2026, and some rungs will have moved by the time you read this. Claims from single labs, single patients and company press releases are marked as such.

Instrument · superhealth

The arithmetic of escape

In 1825 the actuary Benjamin Gompertz noticed a regularity in the tables insurers used. After early adulthood, a person's chance of dying in the coming year grows by the same proportion every year. Today it doubles about every eight years. In a rich country a 40-year-old has roughly a 0.15% chance of dying this year, and an 80-year-old about 4%.

That doubling is why curing diseases one at a time buys so little. Cut everyone's risk of death by a quarter, at every age, and you've made them about three and a half years younger, because that's how long the doubling takes to win the quarter back. In 1990 Jay Olshansky and colleagues estimated that ending every cancer would add about three years to life expectancy at birth. The model below agrees.

A Gompertz–Makeham model (Gompertz 1825; Makeham 1860). Each year's risk of death is a constant risk from outside, plus an aging term that doubles every eight years of biological age, set so that life expectancy at birth is 81, as in a rich country now. Cancer causes roughly a quarter of deaths in rich countries, so curing it cuts the aging term by a quarter. Repair turns biological age back, but never below 20. Real death rates climb more slowly past about 100, babies face extra risk, and no therapy today repairs anything like a year a decade. A toy, to show the arithmetic.

Press Cure all cancer, then Slow aging by a fifth. Ending the biggest killer adds about three years. Slowing the clock that drives it by a fifth adds about seventeen. That's the case for treating aging itself.

What slows the clock now

In mice, several things. Since 2004 the US National Institute on Aging's Interventions Testing Program has run candidate drugs through identical trials at three sites. Rapamycin, started at 20 months, roughly 60 in human terms, extended life by 9% in males and 14% in females (Harrison et al. 2009). Acarbose, 17-α-estradiol and canagliflozin also work, mostly in males. The program also shows how fragile results are. Astaxanthin and meclizine extended male lifespan by 12% and 8% in one round (2024), and showed no benefit when retested at other doses and later starting ages.

In people, little has been measured. In the CALERIE trial, adults asked to eat 25% less managed about 12% less for two years, and one measure of the pace of aging, DunedinPACE, slowed by 2–3% (Waziry et al. 2023). Two other epigenetic clocks didn't move. Set How fast you age to 97% to see what that would buy if it lasted a lifetime: about two years. Dogs may get there first. Loyal's drug LOY-002, for dogs over ten, has had two of the three parts of an application for conditional FDA approval accepted (January 2026), and a large trial is testing whether it extends their lives.

Repair, not only delay

Slowing aging stretches the curve. To escape it, damage has to be repaired faster than it builds up. In 2002 Aubrey de Grey and colleagues sorted the damage of aging into kinds, each with a proposed fix, and de Grey later named the goal longevity escape velocity: therapies that add more than a year of remaining life for every year that passes (2004).

Two kinds of repair have worked in old mice. Drugs that kill senescent cells, which have stopped dividing and inflame the tissue around them, raised old mice's survival after treatment by 36% (Xu et al. 2018). Partial reprogramming uses brief doses of three of the four factors Shinya Yamanaka found could turn adult cells back into stem cells (2006). It restored sight in old mice (Lu et al. 2020), and in one study it more than doubled the median remaining life of male mice treated at 124 weeks, about 77 in human terms (Macip et al. 2024). That result comes from one lab, at the company developing the therapy, and hasn't been independently repeated.

The first person was dosed with a partial reprogramming therapy in June 2026. Life Biosciences' ER-100 is injected into the eye, for damage to the optic nerve, and switched on by an antibiotic. By October, three people with glaucoma had received the lowest dose. None had a serious adverse event by day 56, and two improved on a test of their field of vision. That's three people, with no control group, and the company's own report. It's also twenty years after Yamanaka's paper.

Now press Stop aging at 60. Repair undoes ten years every decade, so biological age stops. Half of these people are still alive at about 140, and a few reach 1,000. Move Repair begins at age down to 30 and half live past 900. What finally kills them is the world: accidents, violence, infection. Set Danger from outside to a tenth and see how much of a very long life becomes a question of safety rather than medicine.

Olshansky and colleagues argued in 2024 that radical life extension this century is implausible: survival to 100 is unlikely to pass 15% for women and 5% for men, unless aging itself can be slowed. Optimists and skeptics agree on that condition. They disagree about whether it will be met.

Instrument · superintelligence

Ten bits a second

The first rung of the mind's ladder is the one we stand on. Writing, numbers, computers and now AI extend what one person can think, and the philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers argue that a notebook you rely on is part of your mind (1998). The effects of AI on human work have now been measured, and they're real but jagged. Customer-support agents with an AI assistant resolved 14% more problems an hour, and novices 34% more (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond 2025). Consultants given GPT-4 did more and better work on tasks inside its abilities, and were 19 percentage points less likely to get one just outside them right (Dell'Acqua et al. 2023). Experienced programmers working on their own large projects took 19% longer with early-2025 AI tools, while believing they'd been 20% faster (METR 2025).

The usual plan for going further is to plug the brain in, and the argument is about bandwidth: we talk to our machines through our thumbs. Measure yours.

English carries about one bit of information per letter, once you allow for how predictable it is (Shannon 1951), so one bit a character is used throughout, as Zheng and Meister do. Mistyped characters don't count. Each step on the scale is a thousand times the last.

In 2024 Jieyu Zheng and Markus Meister added up the information rate of the things people do: typing, speaking, playing fast video games, memorizing cards, solving a Rubik's cube blindfolded. Each comes to about 10 bits a second. Measured another way, speech carries about 39 bits a second, and all 17 languages in one study land near that rate, because the denser ones are spoken more slowly (Coupé et al. 2019). Meanwhile each retina sends the brain something like 10 million bits a second (Koch et al. 2006), and the senses together about a billion.

Brain–computer interfaces have given output back to people who'd lost it. A man with ALS, with 256 electrodes in his speech cortex, talked at about 32 words a minute with 97.5% accuracy over eight months (Card et al. 2024). Another system reached 62 words a minute (Willett et al. 2023). For the people using them, that's a voice returned. But Zheng and Meister's point is that the slow part seems to be central. The senses and muscles run fast, the inner process that decides what to do runs at about 10 bits a second, and nobody knows why. If thinking is the bottleneck, a wider cable won't make anyone think faster. The rung for that, an interface to cognition rather than to the muscles, is missing.

Reading every neuron

The number of neurons scientists can record at once has doubled about every seven years since the 1950s (Stevenson & Kording 2011). Microscopes can now follow the activity of about a million neurons across a mouse's cortex, through a window in its skull (Demas et al. 2021). Human implants listen through hundreds to about a thousand electrodes. Neuralink's has 1,024, and 21 people had one by January 2026. A human brain has about 86 billion neurons (Azevedo et al. 2009). At seven years a doubling, getting from a million to 86 billion takes more than a century.

Better hardware, and a copy

In twin studies, genes account for about half the differences in adult intelligence (Plomin & Deary 2015), and polygenic scores built from DNA now let IVF clinics rank embryos. Choosing the top-scoring of ten embryos is expected to raise IQ by about 2.5 points, with wide error (Karavani et al. 2019). Carl Shulman and Nick Bostrom worked out a much stronger version on paper (2014): make eggs and sperm from stem cells, select, and repeat in the dish, running many generations of selection in months. Mouse eggs have been made from stem cells since 2016 (Hikabe et al.). In 2025 a team in Oregon made human eggs from skin cells by nuclear transfer, and most of the embryos had abnormal chromosomes. Editing many genetic variants directly might become feasible within about thirty years, Peter Visscher and colleagues estimated (2025). Every one of these steps raises questions about consent, inequality and the history of eugenics, which those authors raise themselves.

The top rung of the mind's ladder is a copy: scan a brain finely enough and run it on a computer. Anders Sandberg and Nick Bostrom wrote the roadmap in 2008, and each step now works for a tiny volume. The whole brain of a fruit fly, about 140,000 neurons and 50 million synapses, was mapped in 2024 (Dorkenwald et al.), and a simulation built from that map predicted which neurons start the fly feeding (Shiu et al. 2024), though it left out the electrical synapses and most chemical modulation. One cubic millimetre of human cortex, removed in surgery, holds 57,000 cells and 150 million synapses and filled 1.4 petabytes (Shapson-Coe et al. 2024). At that resolution a whole human brain would come to about 1.7 zettabytes. Even for the worm C. elegans, whose 302 neurons were mapped in 1986, the map alone doesn't predict what it does. Models also need the strength and sign of each connection and the chemicals that modulate them.

Then there's the route to superintelligence that doesn't go through a human brain at all. In a 2023 survey of 2,778 AI researchers, the combined forecast gave a 50% chance that machines would outperform humans at every task by 2047, thirteen years sooner than the same survey a year earlier (Grace et al. 2024). The length of task that AI agents can complete doubled about every seven months from 2019 to 2025 (Kwa et al. 2025). The rung nobody has built is a reliable way to check that a system far more capable than its makers pursues what they meant. If the climb toward augmented human minds is slow and the machine ladder is fast, the order in which the rungs get built may matter more than any one of them.

Instrument · superhappiness

The treadmill inside

The lowest rungs of the mood ladder may be the most important ever built. On 16 October 1846, at Massachusetts General Hospital, William Morton put a patient to sleep with ether, and the surgeon John Collins Warren removed a tumour from his neck. In January 2025 the FDA approved suzetrigine, which blocks Nav1.8, a sodium channel found mostly in pain-sensing nerves: the first new class of pain drug in about two decades. All 21 antidepressants compared in a 2018 analysis of 522 trials beat placebo, modestly (Cipriani et al.). In a 2025 phase 3 trial, one dose of psilocybin beat placebo by 3.6 points on a 60-point depression scale. Real, and smaller than the headlines.

Above those rungs, the ladder is about raising the good, not only removing the bad, and here nature has run some experiments. Jo Cameron, a woman in Scotland, felt little pain in childbirth or surgery, heals unusually fast, and scored zero on a standard anxiety questionnaire. She carries a deletion in a gene the researchers named FAAH-OUT, which turns down an enzyme that breaks down anandamide, one of the body's own cannabinoids (Habib et al. 2019; Mikaeili et al. 2023). People born without a working Nav1.7 channel feel no pain at all, and it's a curse: they burn and break themselves without noticing (Cox et al. 2006). Pain is information. David Pearce's proposal keeps the information and moves the floor: "information-sensitive gradients of bliss," in which tomorrow's lows would feel better than today's highs.

Where would the dial be? In 1954 James Olds and Peter Milner found that rats would press a lever again and again, thousands of times an hour, to stimulate parts of their own brains, and the press called them pleasure centres. Kent Berridge and colleagues have since argued that most of these sites drove wanting, not liking (Berridge & Kringelbach 2015). Wanting runs largely on dopamine and can turn compulsive. Liking seems to depend on small hotspots, one about a cubic millimetre in a rat's nucleus accumbens, where opioid stimulation doubles or triples the facial signs of pleasure at a taste of sugar (Peciña & Berridge 2005). In twin studies, well-being is about 36% heritable (Bartels 2015), spread across many genetic variants, each with a tiny effect (Okbay et al. 2016). Nobody yet knows a target that durably raises the baseline mood of a healthy person without blunting, addiction or loss of drive.

Whatever the dial does has to survive the treadmill. In the 1980s and 1990s Wolfram Schultz recorded dopamine neurons in monkeys. An unexpected drop of juice made them fire. Once a light reliably predicted the juice, they fired at the light, and the juice itself did nothing (Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997). Robb Rutledge and colleagues found the same shape in people. Moment to moment, happiness tracked recent expectations and surprises, not how much people had won, in the lab and in 18,420 players of a smartphone game (2014).

A toy in the spirit of Rutledge et al. (2014). Each moment brings a reward, with a little luck either way, and your expectation learns from each one. How you feel follows recent surprises, the gaps between what you got and what you expected, with losses weighing twice as much as gains (Kahneman & Tversky 1979). Fitted models also give some weight to expectations themselves, which makes the treadmill weaker than it is here. The set point lifts the baseline without touching the reward signal. Nobody knows how to do that in a brain.

Get a raise and the joy fades as you come to expect it. Wire the reward signal and the same thing happens, faster: the boost fades, and when the wire is switched off, the expectation it left behind turns into a crash. That's the shape of tolerance and withdrawal, though this toy is far simpler than real addiction. Raising the set point works in the model because it moves the baseline instead of the signal. Feelings still rise and fall with what happens, so they still carry information. Whether anything in a real brain works like that dial is a missing rung.

The treadmill in people is real, but it's partial. Swedish lottery players who won large prizes were more satisfied with their lives for more than a decade afterwards (Lindqvist, Östling & Cesarini 2020).

Learning as the engine

Press Keep learning new things. Each new skill pays a little more as you master it, and while you're improving, the rewards keep beating your expectations. Your mood stays up as long as there's something left to learn. That's a toy version of an idea with serious backers. In active inference, Karl Friston's account of the brain as a prediction machine, feeling good has been proposed to track how fast prediction error is falling rather than how large it is (Joffily & Coricelli 2013; Van de Cruys 2017; Kiverstein, Miller & Rietveld 2019). Jürgen Schmidhuber proposed that curiosity, fun, art and humour all reward one thing, compression progress: finding a shorter description of what you've seen (2010). Pierre-Yves Oudeyer's robots, rewarded only for progress in learning, taught themselves in stages, the easier things first (Oudeyer, Kaplan & Hafner 2007). Matthew Hurley, Daniel Dennett and Reginald Adams argue that a joke pays you for catching a wrong guess your own mind had slipped in (2011). And curiosity helps memory. People curious about a trivia answer remembered it better, and remembered faces they'd seen while waiting for it (Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014).

Speculation, flagged
  • If good feeling comes from error being reduced, the three supers collide. A mind that predicts everything has nothing left to learn, so superintelligence could undercut superhappiness unless the world, or the mind's own projects, stays open-ended.
  • Active inference has an answer to the opposite worry, the dark-room problem: why doesn't a prediction machine sit in a dark, predictable room? Because organisms expect, and so seek, the states that keep them alive, and they value information for its own sake (Friston, Thornton & Clark 2012).
  • That leaves two bets for superhappiness. Pearce's raises the floor. The learning bet keeps the frontier open. They don't exclude each other, and neither has been tested in a person.
  • These are frameworks, argued seriously. None is a finding.

In 1974 Robert Nozick asked whether you'd plug into a machine that gave you any experiences you chose, for the rest of your life. Most people say no. Whatever superhappiness turns out to be, most of us seem to want it attached to the world.

Instrument · the posthuman

How big can one mind be?

The tops of the ladders vanish into one point. Vernor Vinge argued in 1993 that once there are minds greater than ours, the future past that point can't be foreseen from here. L. A. Paul's argument about transformative experience, on page VIII, applies too: you can't know in advance what you'd value as a posthuman.

Physics still applies at the top. Seth Lloyd worked out in 2000 that a kilogram of matter could in principle perform about 5 × 1050 operations a second, far beyond any brain. But the speed of light caps something else: how big one mind can be and still be one mind, because signals take time to cross it. In your brain, an average fibre linking the two temporal lobes is estimated to take more than 25 milliseconds one way (Ringo et al. 1994). Light crosses the Earth in 42.

Suppose a mind stays a single mind only if a signal can cross it within one of its own moments, and take your 25 milliseconds as a moment. That's an assumption, not a law. Choose how much faster than you it thinks, and how its signals travel.

Largest size = signal speed × 25 milliseconds ÷ speed-up. The 25 milliseconds is a rough one-way time for signals between your temporal lobes (Ringo et al. 1994). Nerve fibres conduct at anything from under 1 to about 100 metres a second, and light in glass travels at about two thirds of its speed in a vacuum. An Earth-sized mind's pace depends only on its signals, not on how fast its parts are.

A mind that thinks a million times faster than you, wired with light, can be about seven and a half metres across: one large room. A mind the size of the Earth, wired with light, can think at most about 0.6 times as fast as you. A mind spread across the solar system would take hours over a single thought. At nerve speed, the largest mind that keeps your sense of now is about 25 centimetres across, a little bigger than your brain.

So a posthuman civilisation probably isn't one vast mind. It's more likely many fast, small ones, sending each other messages that take ages by their own clocks. That is to say, a society. Anders Sandberg made a version of this argument in 1999, for what he called Jupiter brains.

Whether a posthuman would still be you is a separate question. Hans Moravec imagined a brain replaced neuron by neuron with artificial parts while its owner stays awake (1988). David Chalmers argued that if experience survives each small step, it should survive the whole replacement (2010). Derek Parfit thought that what matters isn't identity at all, but continuity of memory and character, which comes in degrees (1984).

The turn

The lowest missing rung

In 1828 the botanist Carl Sprengel saw that a plant's growth is limited not by the total of its nutrients but by the scarcest one, and Justus von Liebig made the idea famous. Ladders work the same way. You climb only as high as the lowest gap, and a rung built above it is a step nobody can stand on yet. In the title, each climber waits at its lowest missing rung, whatever has been built above it. Tap a ladder there to build it.

Zoom out over all three ladders and the gaps turn out to be the same few, in different clothes:

DeliveryGetting a gene, a factor or a drug into every cell of an adult body, or an electrode to every neuron.
MeasurementA biomarker that predicts healthy years. A measure of feeling that isn't a self-report. A test of whether a copy of a mind is a mind.
ControlRejuvenating cells without causing cancer. Pleasure without compulsion. Capability that stays aligned with its makers.
The codeHow a brain represents a fact, a memory or a feeling. Writing to a mind, and copying one, wait on it.

Some rungs have been drawn in detail and wait on engineering. Robert Freitas designed the respirocyte in 1998: a one-micron diamond tank that could carry 236 times as much oxygen as a red blood cell, volume for volume. Eric Drexler worked out machines that build with atomic precision in Nanosystems (1992). Sandberg and Bostrom's emulation roadmap, Shulman and Bostrom's iterated selection and Pearce's gradients of bliss are on paper too. And drawn rungs do get built. CRISPR editing was described in 2012 and became an approved medicine, Casgevy, eleven years later. Yamanaka's factors took twenty years to reach a human eye. A brain implant first moved a cursor in 2006 (Hochberg et al.), and by 2023 implants were turning attempted speech into text at 62 words a minute.

Try this week
  • Find your age in your country's life table and read off the chance of dying this year. Then look eight years further down. It will be about twice as high.
  • Type the line in the bandwidth test again tomorrow, and the day after. Practice will make you faster. Notice whether it makes you think faster.
  • The next time you laugh, ask what you'd been expecting a moment before.
  • Pick a missing rung from the ladder and find the newest paper on it. Is it still missing?

Nobody can see the top of the ladder from here. Everyone can see the lowest missing rung.
