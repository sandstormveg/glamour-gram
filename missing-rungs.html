The Missing Rungs

Superintelligence, superhappiness, superhealth. The road from human to posthuman as it stands: what's built, what's drawn but not yet built, and what nobody knows how to make.

    Superhealth

    The arithmetic of escape

    In 1825 the actuary Benjamin Gompertz noticed that after early adulthood, a person's chance of dying in the coming year grows by the same proportion every year. Today it doubles about every eight years. That doubling is why curing diseases one at a time buys so little. Ending every cancer would add about three years to life expectancy at birth (Olshansky, Carnes & Cassel 1990), and the model below agrees.

    A Gompertz–Makeham model (Gompertz 1825; Makeham 1860). Each year's risk of death is a constant risk from outside, plus an aging term that doubles every eight years of biological age, set so that life expectancy at birth is 81, as in a rich country now. Cancer causes roughly a quarter of deaths in rich countries, so curing it cuts the aging term by a quarter. Repair turns biological age back, but never below 20. Real death rates climb more slowly past about 100, babies face extra risk, and no therapy today repairs anything like a year a decade. A toy, to show the arithmetic.

    Press Cure all cancer, then Slow aging by a fifth. Ending the biggest killer adds about three years. Slowing the clock that drives it by a fifth adds about seventeen. Escape needs more than slowing. In 2004 Aubrey de Grey named the goal longevity escape velocity: therapies that add more than a year of remaining life for every year that passes. Press Stop aging at 60. Half of these people are still alive at about 140, and what finally kills them is the world, not their cells. Set Danger from outside to a tenth and a very long life becomes a question of safety rather than medicine.

    In mice, rapamycin and drugs that clear senescent cells extend life, and partial reprogramming has restored sight (Harrison et al. 2009; Xu et al. 2018; Lu et al. 2020). In people, the first partial-reprogramming therapy was dosed in June 2026, into the eyes of three people with glaucoma. None had a serious adverse event by day 56, but there was no control group, and the report is the company's own. Optimists and skeptics agree that radical life extension needs aging itself to be slowed (Olshansky et al. 2024). They disagree about whether it will be.

    Every year the old tortoise grew one year older, and every year the healers of the forest found a way to give her back three months. “At this rate,” said the hare, “you'll still get old.” “Yes,” said the tortoise. “Until the year they give me back twelve.”

    Superintelligence

    Ten bits a second

    Writing, numbers, computers and AI already extend what one person can think, unevenly. Customer-support agents with an AI assistant resolved 14% more problems an hour, and novices 34% more (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond 2025). Experienced programmers working on their own projects took 19% longer with early-2025 tools, while believing they'd been 20% faster (METR 2025). The usual plan for going further is to plug the brain in, because we talk to our machines through our thumbs. Measure yours.

    The clock starts at your first keystroke.

    English carries about one bit of information per letter, once you allow for how predictable it is (Shannon 1951), so one bit a character is used throughout, as Zheng and Meister do. Mistyped characters don't count. Each step on the scale is a thousand times the last.

    Typing, speaking, fast video games, memorising cards, solving a Rubik's cube blindfolded: Jieyu Zheng and Markus Meister found that each runs at about 10 bits a second, while the senses take in about a billion (Zheng & Meister 2025). Implants have given voices back. A man with ALS talked at about 32 words a minute with 97.5% accuracy over eight months (Card et al. 2024). But the slow part looks central, not in the muscles, and nobody knows why. If thinking is the bottleneck, a wider cable won't make anyone think faster. Meanwhile, surveyed AI researchers gave a 50% chance that machines will beat people at every task by 2047 (Grace et al. 2024).

    A fable · II

    A scribe sat by a river that carried a billion drops past her every second. She could write down ten of them. The king gave her a wider pen, then a wider desk, then a second hand. She still wrote ten. “The river isn't the problem,” she said. “I'm the narrow part, and nobody knows why.”

    Superhappiness

    The treadmill inside

    The lowest rungs of the mood ladder may be the most important ever built. On 16 October 1846, at Massachusetts General Hospital, a patient slept through surgery under ether. Raising the good is harder than removing the bad, because of a treadmill. Dopamine neurons fire for surprises, not for rewards they've learned to expect (Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997). Moment to moment, happiness tracks recent surprises, not winnings, in the lab and in 18,420 players of a smartphone game (Rutledge et al. 2014).

    A toy in the spirit of Rutledge et al. (2014). Each moment brings a reward, with a little luck either way, and your expectation learns from each one. How you feel follows recent surprises, the gaps between what you got and what you expected, with losses weighing twice as much as gains (Kahneman & Tversky 1979). Fitted models also give some weight to expectations themselves, which makes the treadmill weaker than it is here. The set point lifts the baseline without touching the reward signal. Nobody knows how to do that in a brain.

    Get a raise and the joy fades as you come to expect it. Wire the reward signal and it fades faster, and switching the wire off leaves a crash. That's the shape of tolerance and withdrawal, in a toy far simpler than addiction. The treadmill is only partial: people who won large lottery prizes were more satisfied with their lives for more than a decade (Lindqvist, Östling & Cesarini 2020). Press Keep learning new things. While you're improving, the rewards keep beating your expectations. Several researchers have proposed that good feeling tracks how fast prediction error is falling rather than how large it is (Joffily & Coricelli 2013; Schmidhuber 2010). That would make learning the one reward with no treadmill. It's a proposal, not a finding.

    A fable · III

    A man found a lamp and wished for a palace. A year later he was as happy as he'd been in his hut. He wished for a bigger palace, and the same thing happened. For his third wish he asked for something to be curious about, forever. The genie, who had been yawning, sat up.

    The turn

    The lowest missing rung

    In 1828 the botanist Carl Sprengel saw that a plant grows only as far as its scarcest nutrient allows, and Justus von Liebig made the idea famous. Ladders work the same way. You climb only as high as the lowest gap, and a rung built above it is a step nobody can stand on yet. Across mind, mood and body, the gaps turn out to be the same four. The first is delivery: a gene, a drug or an electrode to every cell or neuron. Then measurement, such as a test that predicts healthy years or a measure of feeling that isn't a self-report. Then control: rejuvenation without cancer, pleasure without compulsion, capability that stays aligned. And the code, how a brain stores a fact or a feeling.

    Drawn rungs do get built. CRISPR editing was described in 2012 and became an approved medicine, Casgevy, eleven years later. Yamanaka's factors took twenty years to reach a human eye.

    Try this week
    • Find your age in your country's life table and read off the chance of dying this year. Then look eight years further down. It will be about twice as high.
    • Type the line in the bandwidth test again tomorrow, and the day after. Practice will make you faster. Notice whether it makes you think faster.
    • The next time you laugh, ask what you'd been expecting a moment before.
    A fable · IV

    A climber asked the ladder-maker which rung to build next. “The one you're standing just below,” he said. “The ones above it can wait. You can't reach them anyway.”

    Nobody can see the top of the ladder from here. Everyone can see the lowest missing rung.
