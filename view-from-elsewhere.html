The View from Elsewhere

You can't see the state you're in. To know how you're doing, you need a second position, and time between.

A letter from an earlier you has arrived
Two positions

Less a paradox than a parallax

From one spot, a near star and a far one look alike: two points of light on a flat sky. Since antiquity, the missing shift between them was an argument that Earth stands still. Then in 1838 Friedrich Bessel watched the star 61 Cygni from opposite sides of Earth's orbit, half a year apart, and saw it move against the stars behind it. The whole shift was about 0.6 arcseconds, the width of a coin seen from 8 kilometres. That tiny difference gave the first distance to a star.

The same year, Charles Wheatstone showed that your brain does this all the time. It builds depth from the small differences between what your two eyes see. One view is flat. Two views, compared, have depth.

Your inner life has the same problem. From inside, a mood doesn't look like a mood. It looks like the way things are.

"Life is a train of moods like a string of beads, and, as we pass through them, they prove to be many-colored lenses which paint the world their own hue, and each shows only what lies in its focus."Ralph Waldo Emerson, "Experience" (1844)

Søren Kierkegaard put the timing in his journal in 1843: life is understood backwards, but it must be lived forwards. So you can't fully judge the state you're in from inside it. You need a second position, and time between. This page is about ways to get one.

An experiment to run while you read

Turn this on and the page will interrupt you four times, at random, to ask where your mind is and how you feel. Psychologists call this experience sampling. Your answers stay on this page, and you'll see them beside the wandering mind.

Instrument · the lens

You can't see the lens you're wearing

What never changes fades from view. In 1953 Lorrin Riggs and colleagues used a tiny mirror on a contact lens to hold an image perfectly still on the retina. Within seconds, fine lines faded away. Ignaz Troxler noticed in 1804 that if you fix your gaze, things near the edge of vision dissolve. Galileo made the point about motion in 1632: below decks on a smoothly sailing ship, nothing you do there can tell you whether you're moving.

Try it with colour. Choose a lens, put it on, and keep your eyes on the dot.

Choose a lens, press Put it on, and keep your eyes on the dot.

Your eyes adjust to the light around them, so a steady tint seems to weaken as you look. When it comes off, the adjustment keeps running for a few seconds, and plain colours look shifted the other way. The lens stays on for 32 seconds.

Moods work the same way. Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell called it the hedonic treadmill in 1971: we adapt to good and bad fortune, and our sense of normal moves with us. But adaptation is often incomplete. After unemployment, for example, average life satisfaction tends not to return fully to where it was (Lucas et al. 2004; Diener, Lucas & Scollon 2006). The tint fades from view. That doesn't mean it's gone.

Instrument · forecast yourself

From one state, another is hard to imagine

We're poor at predicting how we'll feel in a state we're not in. George Loewenstein calls it the hot–cold empathy gap: when calm, we underestimate the pull of hunger, fear or craving, and in their grip we can't imagine calm (2005). Timothy Wilson and Daniel Gilbert found that people overestimate how strongly and for how long future events will move them, which they call the impact bias (2005). The philosopher L. A. Paul argues that some experiences are transformative. You can't know what they're like, or what you'll care about afterwards, until you've changed (2014).

Memory edits too. Daniel Kahneman and colleagues found that people judge a past experience mostly by its peak and its end (1993). The self who remembers isn't quite the self who lived it.

Instrument · three networks

A mind that wanders, and one that works

In 2001 Marcus Raichle's team described something odd in brain scans. A set of regions was more active when people rested than when they did a task. They called it the default mode (Raichle et al. 2001). It's busy when you remember, imagine the future, think about yourself or other people, or let your mind wander. When you focus on a task, a central executive network leads and the default mode quietens. A third network, the salience network, seems to help switch between them (Menon & Uddin 2010).

A cartoon of one hemisphere, front to the left. Hollow dots lie on the brain's inner surface or deep inside it. The real networks span both hemispheres. The flash at each change is the salience network.

It isn't a simple switch. Some of the opposition between the networks in scans can come from how the data are processed (Murphy et al. 2009), and in creative thinking the default and executive networks work together (Beaty et al. 2016). Kalina Christoff and colleagues describe mind-wandering as thought moving freely, loosened from goals and habits (2016).

Is wandering good for you? In 2010 Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert used an iPhone app to interrupt 2,250 adults at random moments. Their minds were elsewhere 46.9% of the time, and on average they were less happy then. Wandering to pleasant topics was no happier than staying on task, and wandering to neutral or unpleasant ones was less happy.

So wandering has a cost. But the same networks let you remember, plan, and see yourself from outside, which is exactly the second position this page is after. The question may be less which mode is best than whether you can move between them.

Instrument · a landscape of states

Health is range, with a way home

Several lines of research point the same way. Catherine Heller and colleagues tracked where people went, by phone, for months. On days when they went to more varied places they felt better, and the link was stronger in people whose hippocampus and striatum were more tightly connected (2020). Feelings that change slowly from moment to moment, called emotional inertia, go with depression (Kuppens et al. 2010) and predict its onset in teenagers (Kuppens et al. 2012). Mood may even slow down before tipping into or out of depression, a warning sign borrowed from the study of ecosystems (van de Leemput et al. 2014). A healthy heartbeat is no metronome either. Its rhythm varies in complex ways, and loses that complexity with disease and age (Goldberger et al. 2002).

But range isn't chaos. Moods that swing hard and fast go with distress too (Trull et al. 2008). What seems to matter is flexibility: moving when the moment calls for it, and coming back (Kashdan & Rottenberg 2010).

A cartoon, not a model of a real brain. Heating and slowly cooling to escape a trap is simulated annealing, from physics and computing (Kirkpatrick et al. 1983). Robin Carhart-Harris and Karl Friston propose that psychedelics flatten the brain's landscape of beliefs in a similar way (2019), and Carhart-Harris and colleagues describe some mental illness as canalization, valleys deepened by use (2023). These are serious hypotheses, not settled science.

Notice that heat alone doesn't help. Too hot, and the ball never settles anywhere. The useful move is heat and then cooling: loosen, explore, and let it settle, perhaps somewhere new. People who work with altered states call the settling part integration.

Ways out, and back

Doors to elsewhere

You don't need anything exotic to change state. Here are some doors people use, and what the evidence says, as plainly as we can put it.

Dreams

Every night you leave. In dreaming sleep, frontal control regions quieten while emotional ones run hot (Maquet et al. 1996). In 2021, four labs in the US, France, Germany and the Netherlands put questions to 36 sleeping volunteers. Some, while lucid dreaming, answered simple maths problems with eye movements.

Konkoly et al. 2021

The edge of sleep

People who drifted briefly into the lightest stage of sleep were nearly three times as likely to find a hidden shortcut in a number puzzle: 83% versus 30% for those who stayed awake. Edison is said to have dozed holding steel balls that woke him as they fell. The study used a bottle the same way.

Lacaux et al. 2021

Awe

Vast things make the self feel smaller. Visitors to Yosemite drew themselves smaller than tourists in a city did (Bai et al. 2017), and awe-inspiring videos quietened the default mode in a brain-scan study (van Elk et al. 2019). Astronauts who see Earth whole often describe a lasting shift, the overview effect.

White 1987; Yaden et al. 2016

Nature

A 90-minute walk through green space, compared with one beside a busy road, reduced rumination and activity in a brain region tied to brooding.

Bratman et al. 2015

Stepping back

Talking to yourself by name, or picturing a scene from a distance, cools reactions and makes reasoning wiser (Kross et al. 2014; Grossmann & Kross 2014). So does asking how this will feel in ten years.

Bruehlman-Senecal & Ayduk 2015

Other people

Others see parts of you better than you do. Friends judge your intellect and creativity more accurately than you can, while you know your own anxieties best.

Vazire 2010

Movement

Exercise helps depression. A 2024 review of 218 trials, with about 14,200 people, found moderate benefits from walking or jogging, yoga and strength training, larger when the exercise was more vigorous.

Noetel et al. 2024

Meditation

For recurrent depression, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy lowered the risk of relapse over 60 weeks by about a third (Kuyken et al. 2016). It seems to work partly by teaching you to see thoughts as thoughts (Teasdale et al. 2002). Experienced meditators show less default-mode activity (Brewer et al. 2011).

Hazard ratio 0.69

Substances, told straight

In a 2006 trial, two thirds of volunteers rated a psilocybin session among the five most meaningful experiences of their lives (Griffiths et al. 2006). In treatment-resistant depression, one 25 mg dose beat a 1 mg dose at three weeks (Goodwin et al. 2022). Against the antidepressant escitalopram, a trial found no clear difference on its main measure (Carhart-Harris et al. 2021). Blinding is hard when people can tell what they took, which may inflate results (Muthukumaraswamy et al. 2021). The risks are real, especially with a personal or family history of psychosis or bipolar disorder, and trials exclude those people. In most places these drugs are illegal.

Promising, unproven, not for everyone

What the doors share: each takes you somewhere you can look back from, and each lets you come back.

Instrument · integration

Understood backwards

A state you've left is easier to see, and so is a self you've left. Derek Parfit came to think that personal identity matters less than it seems: what joins you to a future you is a matter of degree, through memory and character. He found this freeing. Once he believed it, he wrote, "the walls of my glass tunnel disappeared" (Reasons and Persons, 1984).

Treating your future self as a real person helps them. Hal Hershfield and colleagues showed people aged images of their own faces, and they put more into a hypothetical retirement fund (2011).

So write to a later you. Mark where you are now, say what you'd want them to know, and seal it. When you come back after the time you choose, it will be waiting. Before you read it, the page will ask where you are then. The gap between the two marks is a view from elsewhere.

    The turn

    No view is the true one

    It's tempting to decide that some other state is the real one: the calm after meditation, the clarity after a journey, the wisdom of age. Emerson saw the catch. Every mood is a lens, and each one "shows only what lies in its focus." Thomas Nagel called the dream of seeing from no particular point the view from nowhere, and argued that we can approach it but never fully reach it (1986). Physics agrees in its own way. In relativity no frame of reference is privileged. The laws are what stays true when you translate between frames.

    A different state isn't wiser just for being different. Depth comes from comparing several, the way two eyes do.

    "I left the woods for as good a reason as I went there. Perhaps it seemed to me that I had several more lives to live, and could not spare any more time for that one."Henry David Thoreau, Walden (1854)

    You can't see your mind from inside. So visit other states, and write home.
